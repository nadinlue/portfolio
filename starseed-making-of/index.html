An Experiment in Making Games

Coming from an art background, I wanted to see how far I could get making a game on my own. I had a specific vision and wanted to find out whether the much-discussed “new tools” could help me turn it into something playable.

I used Claude, ChatGPT and Midjourney alongside Photoshop and my existing experience in game art. The aim was a small, playable experience I would enjoy myself, with no commercial release planned.

I wanted to find out how these tools hold up when you have a specific vision. Where do they help? Where do they become a limitation? And how much direction, iteration and hands-on work does it take to get the result you want?

Creating every piece of artwork myself wasn’t the focus this time. I wanted to shape the whole experience—visuals, gameplay, sound and pacing—and take on the challenges beyond my usual role.

The visual goal was clear: a game with its own identity and a consistent art direction, without the familiar AI-generated look.

The rooftop

The rooftop is multiple painted 4K layers on flat planes in 3D space, from the sky to the wall in front of the camera. Each layer has its own parallax speed: the sky barely moves (5%), the garden at 58%, the foreground at full speed. Props sit on their own planes, so the character can walk behind the telescope, and shadows, glows and fog are added in the engine rather than painted in.

Finding the look

I started with a simple AI-generated rooftop to test the parallax. Once it was in the game, I began shaping it around what I wanted the player to do there. I explored variations with Midjourney and ChatGPT, then brought the pieces into Photoshop to rearrange the layout, paint over details, and bring the lighting and colours together. Each pass let me see what worked in the build and decide what to push further.

Notes

What worked

  • Swapping art: I updated a layer in Photoshop, and Claude Code slotted the new version straight back into the game
  • Exact placement: I exported every layer plus one reference with all layers visible, and everything landed where I had painted it
  • The parallax rig: it needed tuning, but the first setup already felt right

What didn't

  • The first rooftop was cohesive, but every new element drifted in tone and perspective, over-rendered towards a standard mobile-game look
  • Bringing it back took the first iteration as reference, prompts asking for less, and a lot of overpainting by hand

Good to know

  • Claude understood me best through screenshots with notes, reference images and small steps, checked in the browser each time
  • The real shift was asking for my own tools: Behind the Scenes started as a way to check each plane's depth

Light & atmosphere

I wanted a night you can feel: warm lamps against the cool dark, with effects that never compete with the art. Glows, shadows, fog and fireflies are generated from the painting when the game loads, then tuned by eye, slider by slider.

Light off and on

Every ten seconds or so a soft gust sweeps across, left to right. A random wave was too busy; gusts feel like a real night breeze.

What worked

  • Generating glows, shadows and fog from the painting: change the light without repainting
  • A slider for every effect, judged by eye on the real picture

What didn't

  • Brush stamps, stepped tones and a 10 fps boil: painted in a still, flicker in motion
  • A random wave in the fairy lights: too busy, so gusts replaced it

Star screen

Looking up should feel like a small ritual, not a menu. You lean into the telescope, echo a constellation's melody, and search the sky for the next one. It had to stay cozy: wrong taps never reset, a hint shows the next star, and the difficulty grows gently.

Plant creator
